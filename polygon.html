150 km from Semey, Kazakhstan. 456 detonations. A city with no name on any map. Here's what's actually on the record.

01 — The City

It didn't officially exist

The Soviet administrative center for the test site had no public name for over four decades — just a postal code, Semipalatinsk-21. Visitors were routed through Moscow and cleared at checkpoints; getting in could take months. At its peak it held roughly 40,000 people who, on paper, lived nowhere at all.

02 — The First Bomb

The wind was already known

On 29 August 1949, the USSR detonated its first atomic bomb here, codenamed First Lightning. Soviet meteorologists knew rain and wind that morning would carry fallout directly over inhabited villages. The test proceeded on schedule anyway.

NEVER TOLD WHY

In one village caught under that first cloud, roughly 90% of residents absorbed up to 1,400 millisieverts in the following year — nearly 500× a typical annual background dose. No evacuation. No explanation.

04 — The Clinic

Disguised as a livestock disease ward

In 1957 the Soviet Ministry of Health quietly opened a facility near the site named "Anti-Brucellosis Dispensary No. 4" — officially for a cattle disease. Its real job was tracking radiation effects across roughly 100,000 exposed residents. Patients were examined for decades. They were never told their actual diagnosis. The findings went straight to Moscow.

05 — The Mechanism

What radiation does, in plain terms

None of the above is speculative — it follows from mechanisms that are extremely well established. Ionizing radiation carries enough energy to strip electrons as it passes through tissue, producing breaks in the DNA double helix — sometimes both strands at once, the hardest kind of damage for a cell to fix cleanly.

A double-strand break — the hardest DNA damage for a cell to repair without error.

Most breaks get repaired correctly. But repair isn't perfect: a small fraction get stitched back together with an error — a mutation. Most mutations do nothing. Some land in a gene that controls when a cell divides or dies. Enough of the wrong mutations in the wrong genes is the accepted mechanism behind how a normal cell becomes a cancer cell — usually only after years to decades of further damage accumulating on top.

This is why the World Health Organization's cancer agency classifies ionizing radiation as a Group 1 carcinogen — its highest-certainty category, alongside tobacco smoke and asbestos, reserved for causes with conclusive human evidence.

A mutation is just a copying mistake that survived. Cancer is what happens when enough mistakes land in the wrong place. The Polygon is one of the clearest, most tragic illustrations of that mechanism at population scale.
