The Count of Monte Cristo · Chapters XVI–XVII

The Workshop of Abbé Faria

What a mind can still build when everything else has been taken away.

Why this page exists. This is my favorite part of Dumas's novel — not the revenge, not the treasure, but the fourteen years a prisoner named Faria spent turning an empty stone cell into a working laboratory. No equipment, no books, no freedom. He kept doing science anyway. Below is a small catalog of everything he built, in the order Dantès discovers it.

Château d'If, off the coast of Marseille

A tunnel meets the wrong cell

For years, a young sailor named Edmond Dantès has been imprisoned without trial. One night, the wall of his cell gives way — not to a rescuer, but to an old man who has spent over a decade digging in the wrong direction. He introduces himself simply as "Abbé Faria." He expected to find freedom. He found another prisoner instead.

What follows is not a story about escape. It's an inventory of everything one exhausted, brilliant man built in the dark, with his hands, out of nothing.

I
Exhibit I — Engineering, undertaken without instruments

The Fifty-Foot Tunnel

For years, Faria cut through solid rock with a homemade chisel, aiming for the outer wall of the fortress. He had no compass and no drafting tools, so he judged the distance and the angle by instinct alone. He was off by ten feet. The tunnel broke through into the wrong cell — Dantès's, not the outer wall he meant to reach.

A note on the error Real research fails exactly like this. You calculate, you dig for years, and you surface ten feet from where you meant to be. Faria didn't call it wasted work. He treated the miscalculation as new information and kept moving.
II
Exhibit II — Metalwork, no forge

A Toolkit Made From a Bed

Faria owned nothing resembling a tool. So he pried the iron clamp from his own bed frame and, without a forge or a workshop, shaped it into a chisel with a beechwood handle, a pair of tongs, and a lever. Every invention that came after this one started with this single piece of scavenged iron.

A note on the equipment No lab, no budget line, no supply order — and he still built the equipment. The tool always comes after the will to build it, not before.
III
Exhibit III — Astronomy, tracked on stone

A Clock Made of Sunlight

With no clock and no calendar, Faria followed a single beam of sunlight that reached his window each day, marking its path on the stone wall. By reading where the light fell, he could tell the hour more precisely than a mechanical watch — because, as he put it, a watch can break, but the sun and the earth never stop working.

A note on the method This is astronomy done with nothing but patience and a wall. No instrument required — just the discipline to actually look, every day, for years.
IV
Exhibit IV — Chemistry, applied to linen

Paper From a Shirt

Faria had studied under real chemists before his arrest. With no lab in his cell, he still found a way to treat plain linen cloth until it turned smooth and firm enough to write on, almost like parchment. His entire manuscript, chapters written from memory, exists on two shirts prepared this way.

A note on the material Chemistry, to him, wasn't a subject he used to know — it was still something he could do, using the shirt on his own back.
V
Exhibit V — Chemistry, from a sealed chimney

Ink From Soot and Wine

His cell had once had a fireplace, sealed long before he arrived, but the soot on its walls remained. Faria scraped it loose and dissolved it in the wine he was allotted on Sundays, producing usable ink. For lines that needed to stand out, he used his own blood instead.

A note on the source He read the room, literally — and saw raw material where a jailer only saw a bricked-up chimney.
VI
Exhibit VI — Materials, sourced from dinner

Pens Made From Fish Bones

On fasting days, the prison kitchen served whiting fish. Faria kept the cartilage from the fish heads, cut and split it the way a scribe would split a goose quill, and used it to write. He admitted he had come to look forward to those meatless days — they meant a fresh supply of pens.

A note on the supply chain He found a source of materials in the prison's own menu. That's not luck — that's reading every part of your environment as a resource.
VII
Exhibit VII — Metalwork, from a candlestick

Blades From a Candlestick

From an old iron candlestick, Faria forged a small penknife sharp enough to trim a quill, and a longer knife that could serve as a blade if it ever came to that. Same principle as the chisel: everything ordinary in his cell doubled as raw material.

A note on the habit He never stopped seeing objects for what they could become, not just what they were labelled.
VIII
Exhibit VIII — Physics & chemistry, after dark

Light in a Cell With No Lamp

To work after dark, Faria rendered the fat trimmed from his meat rations into oil and burned it in a small clay lamp. For fire, he kept flint and a scrap of cloth as tinder. For matches, he told his jailers he had a skin condition and needed sulfur — they believed him, and gave it to him.

A note on the workaround He rebuilt lighting and fire-starting from first principles, then talked his way past the one ingredient he genuinely could not make himself.
IX
Exhibit IX — Engineering, three years in the making

Thirty Feet of Rope, Hidden in a Wall

Over three years, Faria unraveled his shirts and bedsheets, twisted the threads into rope, and wove a ladder strong enough to hold a grown man's full weight, sewn together with a needle he'd carved from a fishbone. It sat hidden behind a loose stone for years, ready, waiting for a chance that never arrived the way he'd planned.

A note on the wait He finished a serious piece of engineering he might never get to use, purely on the chance it would matter one day. That's what long research looks like from the inside.
X
Exhibit X — The manuscript, sixty-eight strips of linen

A Life's Work on Two Shirts

The tunnel was never Faria's real project. That was a book — a treatise on the possibility of a unified Italian monarchy, an idea he'd carried since before his arrest. He wrote the whole thing from memory, with no reference book in reach, across sixty-eight rolled strips of treated linen. He called it the work of his entire life.

A note on the purpose Certain he might die in that cell, he still wrote something meant to outlive him. That's the whole premise of research — work built for a reader who isn't in the room yet.
Closing note

Why this page exists

What stays with me about Abbé Faria isn't the plan to escape — it's everything he built while he waited for it. Locked in a cell with nothing, he kept doing science anyway: measuring, testing, failing, recalculating, and writing it all down for a reader who might never exist.

He's fictional. The instinct he represents isn't. It's the refusal to let bad conditions become a reason to stop being curious. I think about this list of ten objects whenever an experiment doesn't work, or the resources aren't there, or the timeline runs longer than planned. Faria didn't have a lab. He had a stone room and about a decade. He built one anyway.
