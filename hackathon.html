Building SteppeMind in Five Hours

Team SteppeMind — a technical build log on what we built, and the decisions that made it shippable in time.

HackAlem AI billed itself as the world's largest agentic-AI hackathon — over 2,500 people on-site in Astana, from 21 countries and 129 universities, run as an official Guinness World Records attempt, with OpenAI, NVIDIA, and Arizona State University as technology partners. The whole build happened inside a single five-hour sprint: teams were disqualified if they were away from their workstation for more than 60 minutes total. We went in as Team SteppeMind, working a case brief our own project docs refer to as "AI Sana" — and shipped a product under the same name, turning a vague business-task problem into a scoped MVP before the clock ran out.

01 — The Case

Good problems, unusable descriptions

The case document handed to teams described a familiar failure mode: a business has a real task it would hand to students, but writes it down as a vague paragraph — missing the context, the data available, the constraints, and what "done" even looks like. Student teams can't propose against that. Most of these tasks just die in someone's inbox.

The constraint that shaped everything downstream: whatever we built had to turn a messy, incomplete problem statement into something a team could actually commit to — without inventing information that wasn't there.

02 — What We Built

A task constructor, not a matchmaker

We deliberately did not build an auto-matching system. A business writes a task in free text; an AI step reads it, flags what's missing, and asks at least three clarifying questions before anything gets published. The business edits and manually confirms the final card. Nothing goes live without a human saying yes.

01

Task constructor

Free-text input → completeness check → clarifying questions → editable, human-confirmed card.

02

Readiness score (0–100)

A transparent breakdown of what's filled vs. missing, recalculated after every confirmed edit.

03

Open catalog

Every published task is visible to every team — sortable by readiness, filterable by topic.

04

Manual decisions

Teams submit idea + plan + deadline + prototype link. The business accepts, rejects, or picks several — by hand.

03 — Stack & Constraints

Built to ship, not to impress

With a hard deadline, most of the interesting engineering decisions were about what not to build:

Next.js 16 (App Router) React 19 + TypeScript Tailwind CSS 4 SQLite + Drizzle ORM OpenAI Responses API Zod validation Docker
  • →No hallucinated facts. The AI step can ask questions and flag gaps — it's never allowed to invent details about a business's task.
  • →Graceful without a key. If OPENAI_API_KEY isn't set, a local fallback keeps the whole flow — including the AI step — demoable offline.
  • →No auto-matching. Scoring ranks readiness, not fit. Who works with whom stays a human call, every time.
  • →Scoped hard. No chat, no notifications, no file storage, no vector database, no mobile layout. Out of scope from the first minute, not cut under pressure in hour four.
  • →Security still mattered. Passwords hashed with scrypt, sessions server-side, cookies HttpOnly + SameSite=Lax — baseline hygiene, even on a five-hour build.
04 — What Stuck With Me

The jump from a six-page PDF to one buildable sentence

The hardest part wasn't the integration work — it was the hour before any code existed, turning a multi-page case document into a single sentence we could actually build against: businesses describe tasks badly; make the readiness of a task visible and let humans decide everything that matters. Everything in the stack above follows from holding that sentence steady under time pressure, instead of chasing every feature the case document gestured at.

Scoping under a deadline isn't the boring part before the real engineering starts. It is the engineering — the rest is just typing.
